## 1. Agile Team Charter

### Evidence
[The Agile Team Charter (PDF)](The_Agile_Team_Charter.pdf)

<iframe src="The_Agile_Team_Charter.pdf" width="100%" height="600px"></iframe>

### Reflection

**Situation:** At the start of the MentorMatch project, our team of five needed a shared understanding of how we would work together. MentorMatch is a platform that connects ICT students with learning resources and senior student mentors. We were also balancing it with the PFP362S Entrepreneur Project, so time was a clear constraint from the beginning. We created an Agile Team Charter, last updated on 3 May 2026, to set our purpose, roles, ways of working and expectations before building anything.

**Task:** My task was to contribute to and agree to the charter as a team member, and to take responsibility for my role as Tester. This meant reviewing and debugging code, doing quality assurance and helping optimise the code the developer produced. I also had to follow the working agreements, such as showing up prepared, raising blockers early and giving constructive feedback.

**Action:** I signed the charter and used it as a guide for my work. The Definition of Done (code completed and reviewed, tests passed, documentation updated and Product Owner acceptance) gave me a clear standard to test against, so I knew when a feature could be accepted. Using Kanban on Trello let me see what was ready for testing, and the daily stand-ups and weekly retrospectives gave me regular chances to report issues and raise blockers early. Clear roles, with the Product Owner making final decisions and an escalation path from the team to the Agile Lead, Product Owner and lecturer, meant we knew who to go to when we needed direction.

**Result:** The charter taught me that Agile depends on clear agreements as much as on technical skill. Writing down our values (respect, transparency, accountability, trust and a learning mindset) made it easier to hold myself and my teammates accountable. I also learned that a charter is a living document. Reviewing it at each retrospective meant we could adjust it as our needs changed, particularly with time as our main constraint. In future projects, I would define working hours and response-time expectations in more detail, since that section of our charter was left fairly open.


## 2. User Stories and Tasks

### Evidence

[User Stories (PDF)](User_Stories.pdf)

<iframe src="User_Stories.pdf" width="100%" height="600px"></iframe>

**Trello board:** [Varsity MentorHub | Trello](https://trello.com/b/jRfNEsBb/varsity-mentorhub)

### Reflection

**Situation:** After agreeing on our team charter, the MentorMatch team needed a way to turn the project idea into work we could plan, assign and track. We chose Kanban on Trello, as stated in our charter, and used it to record our user stories and the tasks that came from them. Our stories covered different points of view, including students and tutors, developers, the business side and the development team as a whole.

**Task:** My task was to understand each user story and its tasks so that I could test the work against what the story promised. As Tester, I needed every story to be clear enough to check, with a defined user, a feature and a benefit. I also had to keep track of the status of each card so I knew what was ready to review.

**Action:** We wrote each story in the format "As a [user], I want [feature], so that [benefit]", for example the registration story ("As a Student or Tutor, I want to register an account and log in securely, so that I can access my personalized MentorMatch dashboard"). We then broke the stories into tasks and spread them across the sprint days, with a card for each day (for example Sprint 5, Days 1 to 4). We used colour labels (Design, Planning and Development) to show the type of work, gave each card a due date, assigned team members, and moved cards to Completed once the work was done. The sprints followed the project's natural order, from wireframes and UI mockups in Sprint 5, to database design in Sprint 6, subscription and partner planning in Sprint 7, environment setup in Sprint 8, registration and authentication in Sprint 9, and the tutor profile feature in Sprint 10.

**Result:** I learned that writing a story from the user's point of view keeps the team focused on the value of a feature, not just the technical work, and it gave me a clear standard to test against. Breaking stories into tasks made large pieces of work manageable, and the labels and due dates made it easy to see what stage the project was at. I also learned that the board is only useful if it is kept up to date. For example, a card should have its checklist ticked when work is finished, so the status shown matches reality. In future projects I would keep task checklists current and write acceptance criteria into each story, so that testing is easier to plan.

## 3. Product Backlog

### Evidence

**Product backlog (PDF):** [Product Backlog (PDF)](Product_Backlog.pdf)

### Reflection

**Situation:** Once the team charter and user stories were in place, MentorMatch needed a single, ordered list of everything the project required, from the business case through to the final review. We kept this list on our Trello board as a series of sprint cards, each with a checklist, assigned members and, for the earlier sprints, due dates.

**Task:** My task was to understand the backlog and its order so that I could plan my testing around it. As Tester, I needed to know which items were finished and ready for review, and which were still to come, especially the Testing & Debugging sprint that was planned for later in the project.

**Action:** The backlog followed the natural order of building a product. It started with planning work (the business case, marketing strategy, documentation and presentation preparation), moved into design (wireframes and the database ERD), then business planning (subscription tiers and partners), and then development (environment setup, registration and authentication, tutor profiles, search and booking, messaging) before ending with testing, performance and a final review. Colour labels separated the types of work, each card carried a 5-item checklist, and the whole team was assigned to every card. I used the checklist counts and the Completed ticks to see what stage each item was at. Items I had not yet started, such as Sprint 14 (Testing & Debugging) and Sprint 15 (Performance & Final Review), were still at the end of the list.

**Result:** I learned that a product backlog is only useful when it is ordered by priority and kept up to date. Putting design and database work before development meant developers had something to build from, and leaving testing until after the features existed made sense for my role. I also noticed that the backlog needed regular maintenance. Some cards were ticked as completed while their checklists still showed 0/5, which made the board harder to trust. In future I would tick checklist items as soon as work is done and review the order of the backlog at every planning session.

## 4. Sprint Backlog

### Evidence

**Sprint backlog (PDF):** [Sprint Backlog (PDF)](Sprint_Backlog.pdf)
**Trello board:** [Varsity MentorHub | Trello](https://trello.com/b/jRfNEsBb/varsity-mentorhub)

<iframe src="Sprint_Backlog.pdf" width="100%" height="600px"></iframe>

### Reflection

**Situation:** MentorMatch was delivered over 15 sprints on our Trello board, each with the same structure: one goal and a checklist of five tasks. The sprints followed the order in which the product had to be built. Sprints 1 to 4 covered planning (the business case, marketing strategy, documentation and presentation preparation). Sprints 5 to 7 covered design and business planning (wireframes and UI mockups, the ERD and database design, and subscription and partner planning). Sprints 8 to 13 covered development (environment setup, registration and authentication, tutor profiles, search, filter and booking, messaging and notifications, and ratings with frontend-backend integration). Sprints 14 and 15 covered testing, performance and the final review.

**Task:** My task was to understand what each sprint committed the team to deliver, and to make sure the work could be tested against its goal. As Tester, I needed to know each sprint's tasks and what finished work should look like, so that I could check it against the Definition of Done in our team charter. I also had to take part in the sprint tasks assigned to the whole team.

**Action:** For each sprint we broke the goal into five tasks and tracked them as a checklist on a Trello card, with the whole team assigned. The planning and design sprints, and the early development sprints (5 to 9), were split into daily cards with due dates, so progress could be checked day by day. Later sprints moved to a single card per sprint, with tasks that followed the build order, such as design the UI, build the feature, then test it (for example Sprint 11: design and build search and filter, design and build booking, then test both). Sprint 14 was dedicated to quality work, with tasks for writing test cases, functional testing, debugging, regression testing and finalising, which matched my role most closely. Sprint 15 ended the project with performance analysis, database and frontend optimisation, a final QA review and a close-out sign-off. I used the checklists and Completed ticks to see what was done and what remained.

**Result:** I learned that sprints work best when they are small, specific and ordered. Five clear tasks per sprint made the work manageable, and putting design and database work before development meant developers had something to build from. Daily cards gave us tighter control in the early sprints, and I saw that testing is strongest when it is planned for in each sprint, not left to the end. I also learned that a board only helps if it is kept accurate. Some cards were marked Completed while checklist items were still unticked, such as the microcopy task in Sprint 5 and the 0/5 checklists on later sprints at one stage, which made the status harder to trust. In future projects I would tick tasks as soon as they are finished, test each feature in the sprint it is built, and review the sprint backlog with the team at every planning session.

## 5. Agile Logbook

### Evidence

**Agile logbook (PDF):** [Agile Logbook (PDF)](Agile_Logbook.pdf)

<iframe src="Agile_Logbook.pdf" width="100%" height="600px"></iframe>

### Reflection

**Situation:** Our team charter committed us to regular Agile ceremonies, including a 15-minute daily stand-up and a weekly retrospective. To keep a written record of this, I kept an Agile logbook during the project. Each week had a taskboard (Product Backlog, In Progress and Completed), a four-question walkthrough covering what needed to advance, work off the board, what the team still had to finish and any blockers, and a daily stand-up log. The logbook covers four weeks, from 13 July to 7 August 2026, starting with our project presentation and moving through Sprint 5 (Wireframes & UI Mockups), Sprint 6 (ERD & Database Design) and Sprint 7 (Subscription & Partner Planning).

**Task:** My task was to take part in the stand-ups and record them honestly each day. Every entry answered three questions: what I had completed since the last stand-up, what I planned to complete before the next one, and what was getting in my way. I also had to keep the taskboard and walkthrough in line with our Trello board, so that the logbook gave an accurate picture of the sprint.

**Action:** I wrote an entry for each working day, linking what I completed to the tasks on the Trello cards. For example, in Sprint 5 I recorded creating the UI copy guidelines, drafting the resource card text, writing the microcopy and compiling the UI specification. In Sprint 6 I recorded the data dictionary standards, the database flow diagrams and the exported ERD graphics, and in Sprint 7 the Tier 1 and Tier 2 overview, the feature breakdown, the partnership outreach email and the report chapter. In the first week, the logbook also captured our team's rehearsal, the presentation to the lecturer and the reflection we held afterwards. At the end of the process I also corrected the sprint labels and taskboards in my logbook so that they matched the Trello board.

**Result:** Keeping the logbook taught me that a stand-up is most useful when it is specific. Writing down what I finished and what I planned to do next made it easy to see how each day moved the sprint forward, and the impediments column made me name problems instead of leaving them vague. I noticed that my blockers were often waiting for team feedback or approval, such as feedback on wording choices, final sign-off on the diagram layout and approval of the outreach email. This showed me that approval was often what slowed us down, more than the work itself. I also learned that the logbook is only as reliable as the effort put into keeping it consistent. I left the Monday entries blank, and I reused the same taskboard and walkthrough from Sprint 5 in later weeks, which I had to correct afterwards. My logbook also stops at Sprint 7, although the project ran to Sprint 15. In future projects I would log every day including Mondays, update the taskboard and walkthrough at the start of each sprint, and keep the logbook going until the end of the project.
